# Repeated mosaic assembly of a complex reproductive structure at unequal evolutionary depths in a young thistle radiation

**Target journal:** *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* — Research Article  
**Status:** V8 concept draft for scientific reorganization; not yet a submission freeze  
**Running title:** Mosaic capitulum assembly through time

## Central claim

A complex reproductive structure can be repeatedly assembled from components that carry different evolutionary histories. In a young East-Asian *Cirsium* radiation, capitulum orientation, phyllary posture and involucre stickiness all changed repeatedly, but their changes occupy different relative evolutionary depths and do not repeatedly localize to the same branches. Orientation additionally tracks a specific present-day ecological regime, yet that regime does not persist across the historical uncertainty envelope of the only calendar-bounded orientation event. Thus, present ecological correspondence can identify how a phenotype is currently sorted without necessarily identifying the conditions under which it originated.

## Abstract

Complex phenotypes are integrated in extant organisms, but integration in the present does not imply that their component traits were assembled through one synchronized evolutionary history. We tested how three capitulum components were assembled within a young East-Asian *Cirsium* radiation using public nuclear phylogenomics, authority-backed trait states, topology-aware ancestral reconstruction, present-day occurrence–environment data and bounded palaeoenvironmental scenarios. Thirty-six of 38 sampled Japanese taxon concepts occur in one dominant radiation containing multiple capitulum configurations. Orientation required four to six minimum state changes across 1,000 bootstrap topologies, phyllary posture exactly three and involucre stickiness exactly five. Their topology-only relative-depth profiles differed: phyllary changes were deeper-permissive than stickiness in 1,000/1,000 paired topologies and deeper than orientation in 993/1,000, while orientation was deeper than stickiness in 905/1,000. Zero of three trait pairs passed a robust shared-transition-localization rule, supporting mosaic rather than synchronized assembly. Orientation provided the strongest ecological bridge: upward-to-downward transition probability tracked a fixed present niche regime of higher precipitation seasonality and lower annual temperature, and the composite correspondence remained exceptional after linear geographic residualization and internal-edge-only scoring. However, the same regime matched only 99 of 376 chronology-by-region scenarios for the sole calendar-bounded upward-to-downward event. These results separate three levels of inference: repeated phenotypic assembly, present ecological sorting and historical cause. The first two are identifiable with current public data for at least one component, whereas the third remains substantially less resolved.

**Keywords:** *Cirsium*; capitulum; mosaic evolution; ecological sorting; evolutionary depth; rapid radiation; phylogenetic uncertainty; trait evolution

# Introduction

Complex reproductive structures function as integrated wholes while simultaneously exposing distinct component traits to different ecological interfaces. Floral orientation can alter presentation and abiotic exposure, involucral architecture can alter physical access to reproductive tissues, and surface secretions can alter interactions with arthropods. Because these components coexist in the same organ, it is tempting to interpret their present association as evidence of a shared evolutionary syndrome. Yet present integration and historical assembly are different biological properties. Traits that operate together today may have arisen at different times, changed on different branches and responded to different ecological pressures.

This distinction is especially important in rapid radiations. When many lineages diversify over short internodes, large phenotypic differences can accumulate within a shallow phylogenetic framework while incomplete lineage sorting, introgression and uneven trait coverage leave uncertainty about the exact placement of individual changes. Under these conditions, asking whether a whole organ is conserved or labile can be less informative than asking how its components were assembled. Three quantities need to be separated. **Recurrence** asks how many changes are minimally required by the observed states. **Evolutionary depth** asks whether those changes are restricted to recent terminal branches or can lie deeper within the radiation. **Shared localization** asks whether different components repeatedly change on the same branches. Agreement among these quantities would support a synchronized history; disagreement would support mosaic assembly.

A second distinction concerns ecology. Contemporary trait–environment correspondence is often used to motivate evolutionary explanations, but a present association can arise through several routes: the same environment may have driven the original transition and continued to maintain it; a trait may have originated under one condition but later been sorted or maintained by another; environmental variables may track a different causal mediator; or the same phenotype may have different functional consequences in different lineages. Therefore, a strong present ecological association should not automatically be projected backward as the historical cause of a transition. An explicit test requires at least a bounded trait event, a defensible palaeolocation range and a historical environmental reconstruction across the entire uncertainty envelope.

The thistle genus *Cirsium* is useful for separating these levels of inference. Public nuclear phylogenomics recovered a large, young Japanese radiation with substantial gene-tree discordance, while independent East-Asian phylogenomic work provides local divergence-time constraints and documents reticulate and cytological complexity. The capitulum contains multiple, readily distinguishable traits, including head orientation, phyllary posture and involucral stickiness. These components differ in state coverage and plausible function, allowing their histories to be reconstructed separately rather than collapsing the head into a single categorical syndrome.

The broader phenomic programme motivating this work first asked how capitulum phenotypes are distributed across present-day geographic and environmental space. The present study asks the orthogonal historical question: **how were those component phenotypes assembled through evolutionary time?** Importantly, the historical analysis does not require a trait to be significant in the contemporary spatial analysis before it is admitted. The connection between the two studies is therefore conceptual rather than a significance-filtered data pipeline: one resolves phenotype across space, the other resolves phenotype through history.

Here we address four questions. **First**, did multiple capitulum configurations arise within a single young radiation, and how many state changes are minimally required for orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness? **Second**, do these components occupy similar evolutionary depths and repeatedly localize changes to the same branches, as expected under a synchronized whole-capitulum history? **Third**, for orientation, the component with the strongest public ecological resolution, do inferred transition directions track a specific present-day environmental regime after accounting for state frequency, phylogenetic placement and simple geography? **Fourth**, does that present regime persist across chronology and palaeolocation uncertainty around the best-bounded historical orientation event? Together, these tests distinguish repeated assembly, current ecological sorting and historical cause rather than treating them as interchangeable evidence for adaptation.

# Results architecture

## 1. Multiple capitulum configurations occur within one young radiation

Thirty-six of 38 sampled Japanese taxon concepts occur within the dominant radiation. Authority-backed states reveal multiple combinations of orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness within that radiation. Therefore, current capitulum diversity is not explained simply by contrasts among deeply separated colonization histories. The central evolutionary problem lies within the radiation: a broadly shared ancestry contains multiple combinations of reproductive-head traits.

**Interpretation:** the radiation provides the appropriate scale for asking how a complex organ was reassembled, rather than treating each phenotype as a marker of a deep lineage.

## 2. Three capitulum components changed repeatedly but at unequal evolutionary depths

Orientation requires six changes on the maximum-likelihood tree and four to six across 1,000 ultrafast-bootstrap topologies. Phyllary posture requires exactly three changes across the same topology ensemble, and stickiness exactly five. These values are minimum-change lower bounds rather than counts of independent adaptive origins.

The histories also differ in relative lineage depth. Bootstrap-median depth envelopes are 0.795–0.994 for orientation, 0.695–1.000 for phyllary posture and 0.937–0.954 for stickiness, where one represents a terminal edge and lower values permit deeper placements. Direct comparison on the same 1,000 topologies places phyllary deeper-permissive than stickiness in 1,000/1,000 topologies, deeper than orientation in 993/1,000 and orientation deeper than stickiness in 905/1,000. Coverage-matched masking preserves the central phyllary-deeper ordering while showing overlap in the strict deepest tails.

**Interpretation:** the three components are not merely different in how many times they changed; they are embedded at different depths of the radiation. Phyllary posture retains the greatest potential for deeper differentiation, orientation spans mixed internal-to-terminal histories, and stickiness is concentrated toward shallower history under current coverage.

## 3. The three component histories are not repeatedly synchronized

Zero of three pairwise comparisons passes the robust shared-transition-localization rule across branch-length-aware and topology-only treatments. The result does not prove genetic or developmental independence, but it rejects the simplest model in which orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness repeatedly shift together as one whole-capitulum state.

**Interpretation:** capitulum diversity is best described as **mosaic assembly**. The present organ is integrated functionally, but its components carry partially distinct histories.

## 4. Orientation transitions track a present ecological regime

Orientation provides the strongest bridge from history to ecology. In the East-Asian occurrence panel, downward/nodding taxa occupy higher precipitation-seasonality and lower-temperature niches than upward/erect taxa with stable direction across accepted topologies and species leave-one-out fits. At the transition level, a fixed upward-to-downward present-niche vector (`BIO15 up + BIO1 down`) is exceptional among exhaustive count-preserving state maps: 16/792 at n>=5, 19/1,716 at n>=3 and 4/126 under the strict n>=10 panel. The reverse transition tracks the opposite side of the same regime. The upward-to-downward signal remains exceptional after linear latitude/longitude residualization (5/126), internal-edge-only scoring (3/126) and their combined stress (3/126).

**Interpretation:** orientation is not randomly distributed with respect to present ecological niche under the declared reconstruction. The supported object is a composite regime rather than one single climatic axis. This is evidence of present ecological sorting associated with transition direction, not evidence that BIO15 or BIO1 itself caused the historical transition.

## 5. The present orientation regime does not identify the origin regime

Only one current orientation event can be linked through the full public chain from trait transition to bounded chronology, palaeolocation scenarios and historical environment. Across 94 admissible chronology pairs and four regional scenarios, the fixed present orientation regime matches only 99/376 historical scenarios. No tested BIOCLIM direction, level, absolute change or temporal variability survives the full uncertainty envelope. Broader lineage-context and global sea-level screens likewise fail to recover a recurring coarse historical trigger.

**Interpretation:** present ecological correspondence and historical origin are decoupled at the resolution of current public data. This does not imply environmental irrelevance. It instead limits which historical story can be claimed from the available evidence.

# Discussion

## A complex reproductive structure was assembled mosaically through time

The strongest result is not a climate association but the historical organization of the capitulum itself. Three traits within the same reproductive head repeatedly changed within a young radiation, yet they occupy different relative depths and do not repeatedly localize transitions to the same branches. This combination is difficult to reconcile with a single persistent whole-capitulum syndrome. Instead, the head appears to have been reassembled through partially distinct component histories.

This result clarifies what “integration” can and cannot mean in a macroevolutionary setting. A capitulum must function as one reproductive unit in every extant plant, but functional integration at the present does not require every component to have changed simultaneously. The history recovered here therefore sits between two extremes. The data do not support one conserved synchronized capitulum history, but neither do they establish complete developmental or genetic independence among traits. Mosaic assembly is the narrower and better-supported description.

The unequal depth profiles add biological content beyond recurrence counts. Stickiness is concentrated toward relatively shallow history, whereas phyllary posture admits deeper placements and orientation occupies an intermediate mixed profile. These differences suggest that the ecological interfaces represented by the components have not been reorganized at the same historical scale. Importantly, the current tree is not dated, so this pattern should be interpreted as relative lineage depth rather than chronological age or rate.

## Ecological interpretation: different components plausibly mediate different interfaces

The historical mosaic becomes biologically intelligible when each trait is treated as an interface rather than as one generic “floral adaptation.” Public functional evidence currently supports three different leading mechanism classes. Orientation is most consistent with an **abiotic reproductive-exposure filter**: close Asteraceae manipulation links head angle to wetting/UV-B exposure, pollen viability and achene production, whereas a simple static pollinator-preference explanation is not required. Phyllary posture is most consistent with a **mechanical access filter**: close Cardueae manipulations show that involucral structures can restrict damaging or illegitimate visitors, direct *Cirsium* evidence links antagonist access to seed loss, and naturally appressed focal *Cirsium* heads remain accessible to legitimate visitors. Stickiness is best treated as an **arthropod-community filter** rather than universal defence: direct *Cirsium* experiments contain both benefit and null contexts, and different arthropod guilds contact, avoid or bypass sticky structures differently.

These mechanism assignments are not three demonstrated East-Asian adaptations. Their value is explanatory: they show why one capitulum can plausibly contain traits that respond to different ecological dimensions and therefore accumulate different histories. A single head simultaneously faces precipitation and radiation, pollinator access, seed predators, florivores, ants, sap feeders and predatory arthropods. If the relative importance of these interactions varies among lineages and places, selection on the head need not act as one coherent syndrome. The observed mosaic history is therefore compatible with a **selection mosaic across ecological interfaces**, while direct selective causation remains to be tested.

## Orientation shows ecological tracking, but current niche is not a fossil of origin

Orientation provides the clearest demonstration of the distinction between present ecological sorting and historical cause. The fixed upward-to-downward transition regime is unusual among exhaustive alternative state maps and survives removal of simple linear geography and terminal-edge contribution. This makes the present ecological pattern more than a raw species-mean contrast. Yet the same regime is not recovered across the historical uncertainty envelope of the best-bounded transition.

There are several biologically different explanations for this decoupling, and current public data do not select among them. The orientation state may have originated under a different environmental combination and later been maintained or sorted along the present regime. The measured BIOCLIM variables may be coordinates of a broader niche rather than the causal mediator itself. The relevant mechanism may depend on local rainfall events, wetting duration, radiation exposure or phenological timing that are not reconstructed by coarse palaeoclimate surfaces. Finally, different lineages may reach a similar orientation state through different ecological routes. The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that environment failed to matter, but that **present ecological correspondence cannot be treated as a direct fossil of historical selective cause**.

This distinction is important beyond *Cirsium*. Comparative studies often move from a robust extant trait–environment association to an adaptive historical narrative without directly testing whether the same regime existed at transition origin. The present analysis shows how those claims can be separated empirically: present correspondence can be real and reproducible while origin conditions remain unidentified.

## Connection to the present-space phenomic study

The companion Azami analysis and EAzami address the same biological object on orthogonal axes. Azami asks how a multidimensional capitulum phenotype is distributed across current geographic and environmental space, explicitly retaining within- and among-taxon variation. EAzami asks how selected, independently defined capitulum components are distributed through evolutionary history. The synthesis is therefore:

`phenotype across present space`  
`+ phenotype through evolutionary time`  
`= a complex organ that is multidimensional in the present and mosaically assembled in history`.

The connection should not be framed as “Azami found a significant trait, therefore EAzami reconstructed it.” EAzami’s headline results—recurrence, evolutionary depth and shared localization—stand independently from Chapter 1 significance. The present-space analysis contributes a complementary scale: it shows that contemporary phenotype structure itself depends on biological scale, while EAzami shows that present integration does not collapse into one shared historical trajectory. Orientation is the strongest explicit bridge because it can be compared across contemporary within/among structure, East-Asian state ecology and evolutionary transition history.

This division also keeps the two papers non-redundant. Azami is fundamentally a **phenotypic geography** paper. EAzami is a **phenotypic history** paper. Their common dissertation-level message is that the capitulum cannot be represented adequately by one species-level syndrome in either space or time.

## The next doctoral task is causal discrimination, not more public-data pattern mining

EAzami leaves three causal gaps that public data cannot close. Those gaps define the experimental programme rather than merely appearing as generic future directions. First, repeated state reconstruction does not identify independent origin under reticulation; own Japan-wide nuclear data, population ancestry, cytotype and same-individual phenotypes are required to distinguish lineage-specific change, ancestral sorting and introgression. Second, ecological correspondence does not identify mechanism; focal manipulations must alter the trait itself while measuring the relevant interaction and reproductive fitness. Third, even a focal fitness effect does not automatically explain every historical transition; function and origin must be demonstrated as separate links.

The current doctoral execution therefore follows the evidence gaps exposed by EAzami. For orientation, the critical test is a direct within-*Cirsium* angle manipulation linked to actual wetting/rain and UV-B exposure, pollen viability, effective pollen transfer and mature viable achenes. For phyllary posture, the test is reversible access/posture manipulation linked separately to legitimate visitation, antagonist access or oviposition, damage and viable seed production. For JPN15 stickiness, the key experiment is within-species neutralization versus sham across local arthropod communities, measuring guild-specific responses and final seed fitness. These experiments test the leading ecological interfaces generated by the public-data synthesis without treating the synthesis itself as proof of adaptation.

At the dissertation level, the programme therefore forms an ordered evidence ladder:

`Azami: phenotype across space`  
`→ EAzami: phenotype through evolutionary history`  
`→ own ancestry data: which historical alternatives are real?`  
`→ focal manipulations: what does each trait do?`  
`→ reproductive fitness: when is that function selectively relevant?`.

This sequence is stronger than starting from a preferred adaptive story. Each stage removes a different class of alternative explanation.

# Conclusion

A young *Cirsium* radiation contains repeated changes in multiple capitulum components, but those components were not assembled through one synchronized history. Orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness occupy different relative evolutionary depths and do not repeatedly share transition locations, supporting mosaic assembly of the reproductive head. Orientation additionally tracks a specific present ecological regime, showing that at least one repeatedly changing component is ecologically organized rather than randomly distributed. Yet that present regime does not persist across the historical uncertainty envelope of the best-bounded origin event. The resulting picture is therefore neither a purely neutral mosaic nor a simple adaptive syndrome: a complex reproductive structure has been repeatedly reassembled through components that plausibly mediate different ecological interfaces, while the historical causes of those changes remain only partly identifiable. The next step is not broader correlation searching but direct ancestry-linked, trait-specific mechanism and reproductive-fitness tests.
