# Mosaic capitulum evolution across distinct ecological interfaces in a young thistle radiation

**Target journal:** *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* — Research Article  
**Status:** V9 ecology-positive manuscript draft; scientific claims restricted to currently frozen EAzami evidence  
**Running title:** Ecological interfaces and capitulum mosaic evolution

## Abstract

Complex reproductive structures are often treated as integrated adaptive syndromes, but different components of the same organ can mediate different ecological interactions and therefore need not share one evolutionary history. We tested this idea in a young East-Asian *Cirsium* radiation by combining public nuclear phylogenomics, authority-backed capitulum states, transition-level present-niche analysis and a homology-restricted synthesis of functional experiments. Thirty-six of 38 sampled Japanese taxon concepts occur in one dominant radiation containing multiple capitulum configurations. Capitulum orientation required four to six minimum changes across 1,000 bootstrap topologies, phyllary posture exactly three and involucre stickiness exactly five. The three histories occupied unequal relative lineage depths, with phyllary deeper-permissive than stickiness in 1,000/1,000 paired topologies and deeper than orientation in 993/1,000; zero of three trait pairs passed a robust shared-transition-localization rule. Orientation additionally showed a repeatable ecological pattern: upward-to-downward transition probability tracked a composite present niche of higher precipitation seasonality and lower annual temperature, with exact finite-map ranks of 4/126 in the strict panel and 3/126 after combined geography-residualized and internal-edge stress. Functional evidence points to different ecological interfaces for the three traits. A close Asteraceae angle manipulation supports protection of reproductive tissues from wetting and UV-B exposure; close Cardueae structure manipulations and direct *Cirsium* antagonist studies support mechanical control of damaging access by phyllaries; and direct *Cirsium* neutralization and arthropod observations support context-dependent, guild-selective filtering by sticky involucres. Independently, experimental reduction of reproductive insect herbivory across four *Cirsium* study programmes increased viable or mature seed output 2.674-fold (95% CI 2.388–2.993). Together, these results support an ecological interpretation in which a single capitulum is assembled from components that repeatedly evolve while mediating distinct abiotic and biotic interfaces. We interpret this pattern as mosaic ecological evolution of a complex reproductive structure, without inferring that every reconstructed change represents an independent adaptive origin.

**Keywords:** *Cirsium*; capitulum; ecological interface; floral herbivory; mosaic evolution; phylogenetic uncertainty; rapid radiation; trait evolution

# Introduction

Complex reproductive structures must work as integrated wholes, yet the traits that compose them often interact with different parts of the environment. Floral orientation can alter exposure to rain, radiation and pollinators; protective bracts and spines can alter access by both mutualists and antagonists; sticky surfaces can trap, deter or redirect arthropods. Because these traits coexist in a single organ, it is tempting to describe the organ as one adaptive syndrome. That interpretation is only justified, however, if its components share sufficiently similar evolutionary and ecological histories.

Work on phenotypic integration and modularity has emphasized that covariance among extant traits need not imply a single underlying developmental or evolutionary process (Klingenberg, 2014; Goswami et al., 2014; Felice & Goswami, 2018; Zelditch & Goswami, 2021). The same problem applies to ecological function. A reproductive structure may simultaneously face several selective agents whose relative importance changes among places and lineages. Pollinators, florivores, predispersal seed predators and abiotic stress can generate reinforcing, opposing or context-dependent selection on different parts of the same flower or inflorescence. Multi-agent manipulative studies therefore often recover a selection mosaic rather than a universally dominant selective agent.

Rapid radiations are particularly informative for testing whether a complex structure evolves as one unit or is repeatedly reassembled from partly distinct components. When substantial phenotypic diversity is compressed into a shallow phylogenetic history, component traits can be compared within broadly shared ancestry rather than only across deeply separated clades. If different components repeatedly change at different positions and depths in the radiation, a whole-organ syndrome becomes a poor historical description even if the components remain functionally integrated in extant plants.

The thistle genus *Cirsium* provides a useful system for this problem. Broad nuclear phylogenomics recovered rapid Pleistocene radiations in the genus, including a dominant Japanese radiation with extensive gene-tree discordance (Moreyra et al., 2025). Independent East-Asian phylogenomic work further documents recent divergence, cytological heterogeneity and reticulate history. The capitulum itself is a composite reproductive structure whose components vary strongly among lineages. Head orientation ranges from erect or upward-facing to nodding or downward-facing. Phyllaries surrounding the head differ in posture and armature. Involucres may be strongly sticky or effectively nonsticky. These traits are homologous components of the same organ but plausibly regulate different ecological interfaces.

Existing functional work makes that possibility biologically concrete. In a close Asteraceae system, experimentally changing capitulum orientation altered achene production, while water and UV-B exposure reduced pollen viability and no static pollinator preference between manipulated angles was detected. In Cardueae, removing involucral spines increased access by illegitimate visitors and reduced filled seed production. Within *Cirsium*, predispersal insect herbivory can impose large reproductive costs, and sticky involucral exudates show benefit in some contexts, no detectable benefit in others and different effects among arthropod guilds. These findings argue against reducing capitulum ecology to one generic axis such as “pollinator attraction” or “defence”.

Here we ask whether the evolutionary history of the capitulum is consistent with that multi-interface ecological view. We combine trait-history reconstruction with functional evidence under a deliberately conservative claim hierarchy. First, do orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness each show repeated change within the young radiation? Second, do the three traits occupy similar evolutionary depths and repeatedly localize changes to the same branches? Third, does the best-resolved trait, orientation, show a transition-level relationship with present ecological niche? Fourth, do independent functional experiments support distinct ecological interfaces for orientation, phyllaries and stickiness, and is the antagonist pathway strong enough to matter for reproductive fitness in *Cirsium*?

Our goal is not to claim that every reconstructed state change is an independent adaptive origin. Instead, we test a narrower ecological proposition: **a complex reproductive structure can be historically reassembled from traits that repeatedly change while mediating different abiotic and biotic interfaces**. This proposition makes a positive prediction about the organization of evolutionary history without requiring a single universal historical driver.

# Materials and Methods

## Nuclear scaffold and focal radiation

The focal historical panel followed the 38 Japanese paper taxon concepts represented in the broad phylogenomic study of Moreyra et al. (2025). We used an independently reconstructed Compositae1061-compatible nuclear phylogram rather than digitizing the published tree. A frozen 241-locus starting set yielded 236 quality-controlled nuclear loci, 176 rootable with the safflower outgroup, and a concatenated alignment of 161,654 bp. Maximum-likelihood inference used IQ-TREE 2 with ModelFinder, 1,000 ultrafast-bootstrap replicates and 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates. Branch lengths were treated as substitutions per site, not calendar time.

Thirty-six of the 38 sampled Japanese concepts occur in the dominant radiation. Multiple authority-backed capitulum configurations occur within this radiation, so the focal phenotype diversity is not restricted to comparisons among deep colonization histories.

## Authority-backed capitulum states

We analysed three discrete traits for which botanical states could be linked to exact paper concepts using authority-quality sources: capitulum orientation, phyllary posture and involucre stickiness. Missing and ambiguous records remained unresolved rather than being imputed. Final historical coverage was 20 concepts for orientation, 10 for phyllary posture and 13 for stickiness.

Orientation and stickiness were represented as binary state systems for the historical analyses, whereas phyllary posture retained its source-defined multistate ontology. Continuous image variables were not discretized to manufacture additional historical coverage.

## Minimum-change reconstruction and evolutionary depth

For the maximum-likelihood topology and each of 1,000 raw bootstrap topologies, we calculated the unordered parsimony minimum for each trait. These values are lower bounds on the number of state changes required by a topology and observed tip states; they are not counts of independent origins, adaptive convergence events or rates.

We next calculated exact bounds on mean relative lineage depth across all globally minimum-cost Sankoff histories. For an admitted tree with \(N\) tips and an edge subtending \(d\) descendants,

\[
D=\frac{N-d}{N-1}.
\]

Terminal edges therefore equal one, whereas smaller values identify edges subtending broader descendant lineages. The metric is topology-based and was not interpreted as calendar age.

Trait depths were compared directly on the same 1,000 bootstrap topologies. A separate masking sensitivity equalized observed-state coverage to test whether the central depth ordering arose trivially from different numbers of resolved concepts.

## Shared-transition localization

An equal-rates Mk diagnostic estimated branch-wise transition probability and excess over the branch prior. Pairwise Spearman correlations compared orientation–phyllary, orientation–stickiness and phyllary–stickiness localization. We paired the branch-length-aware maximum-likelihood result with a topology-only sensitivity in which non-root branches were assigned equal length. A pair passed the descriptive robust shared-localization rule only when positive localization persisted under both treatments.

## Orientation present-niche analysis

Orientation had the strongest state-diverse public ecological coverage. Spatially thinned occurrence records with complete CHELSA extraction were summarized to taxon environmental centroids. The fixed upward-to-downward present-niche vector was higher precipitation seasonality and lower annual mean temperature (`BIO15 up + BIO1 down`).

For each accepted topology, a symmetric two-state CTMC provided edge probabilities of upward-to-downward and downward-to-upward change. Brownian reconstruction of standardized taxon environmental centroids supplied branch environmental differences. We calculated a transition-weighted composite statistic for the fixed BIO15/BIO1 vector.

Every count-preserving orientation state map was enumerated. The observed statistic was ranked within the complete finite set for three occurrence-coverage panels: n≥5, n≥3 and a strict n≥10 panel. Fixed falsifications tested whether the signal was reducible to linear latitude/longitude structure or terminal-edge contribution. Exact finite-map fractions are descriptive conditional ranks, not biological-replicate P values.

## Functional evidence hierarchy

We synthesized functional evidence according to similarity in organ, manipulation geometry and taxonomic context. Evidence from direct *Cirsium* experiments was prioritized over broader analogues when available; close Cardueae or Asteraceae manipulations were used when no direct focal-genus manipulation was recovered. Effect sizes from external taxa were not transported to East-Asian *Cirsium*.

For orientation, the closest direct manipulation compared natural nodding and artificially erect capitula in *Cremanthodium campanulatum* (Asteraceae; DOI 10.1080/17550874.2012.702793), measuring achene production, pollen vulnerability to water and UV-B, pollinator preference and internal floral temperature.

For phyllary architecture, the closest structural manipulation was removal of involucral spines in *Centaurea solstitialis* (Cardueae; DOI 10.2307/3672545). We combined this with direct *Cirsium* evidence linking predispersal antagonist access to reproductive loss and with focal *Cirsium sieboldii* observations showing that naturally appressed phyllaries remain accessible to legitimate visitors.

For stickiness, we prioritized direct *Cirsium* neutralization and natural-history studies. Willson, Anderson & Thomas (1983) experimentally occluded sticky bracteal exudates in *C. discolor* and *C. flodmani*. Thomas (2003; DOI 10.22543/0090-0222.2085) conducted later neutralization tests in *C. discolor*, and Thomas (2007; DOI 10.22543/0090-0222.2188) documented guild-specific arthropod use of sticky inflorescences.

## Reproductive-herbivory meta-analysis

To quantify whether the antagonist pathway has sufficient magnitude to affect reproductive fitness in *Cirsium*, we used the frozen random-effects meta-analysis of experiments reporting viable or mature seed output under experimentally reduced versus ambient insect herbivory. The estimand was

\[
RR=\frac{\text{seed output under reduced herbivory}}{\text{seed output under ambient herbivory}}.
\]

Nine within-study contrasts were collapsed to four independent data-generation programmes: Louda & Potvin (1995), Maron et al. (2002), West & Louda (2018) and Russell et al. (2025), covering *C. canescens* and *C. occidentale* var. *occidentale*. Multiple contrasts from the same programme were collapsed before across-study pooling.

## Multi-agent selection synthesis

A separate registry of manipulative floral-selection studies asked whether one selective-agent class dominates across systems. Five strict factorial programmes manipulated both pollination and antagonism with reproductive-fitness endpoints. We classified each programme as antagonist-dominant, pollinator-dominant, or mixed/trait-dependent using prespecified criteria. This synthesis was used to interpret ecological context, not to estimate a pooled selection coefficient for *Cirsium*.

# Results

## A young radiation contains repeated reassembly of capitulum traits

Thirty-six of 38 sampled Japanese concepts belong to the dominant radiation, yet multiple combinations of orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness occur within it. The diversity to be explained is therefore largely embedded within a young common historical framework rather than partitioned only among deep lineages.

All three focal components required repeated changes. Orientation required six changes on the maximum-likelihood tree and four to six across the 1,000 bootstrap topologies, with a median of five. Phyllary posture required exactly three changes across the topology ensemble. Stickiness required exactly five changes.

Repeated change was therefore a property of multiple capitulum components rather than one exceptional trait.

## The three traits occupy unequal evolutionary depths

The bootstrap-median relative-depth envelopes were 0.795–0.994 for orientation, 0.695–1.000 for phyllary posture and 0.937–0.954 for stickiness. Stickiness was concentrated toward relatively shallow placements, whereas phyllary posture admitted substantially deeper placements and orientation occupied an intermediate mixed range.

Paired comparison on the same 1,000 bootstrap topologies strengthened this ordering. Phyllary was deeper-permissive than stickiness in 1,000/1,000 topologies and deeper than orientation in 993/1,000. Orientation was deeper-permissive than stickiness in 905/1,000, with seven ties. The complete central ordering `phyllary < orientation < stickiness`, where lower values indicate deeper permitted histories, occurred in 898/1,000 topologies.

Coverage-matched masking retained the central phyllary-deeper pattern while showing overlap in the strict deepest tails. The supported result is therefore unequal central evolutionary depth, not complete separation or a difference in dated evolutionary rate.

## Capitulum components do not repeatedly change as one synchronized syndrome

Zero of three pairwise trait comparisons passed the robust shared-transition-localization rule. Orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness therefore did not repeatedly concentrate transition probability on the same branches under both branch-length-aware and topology-only treatments.

Combined with their unequal depth profiles, this result supports **mosaic historical assembly** of the capitulum: components of one reproductive structure repeatedly changed, but not as one synchronized historical unit.

## Orientation transitions track a present ecological regime

Orientation provided the strongest trait-specific ecological signal. The fixed upward-to-downward composite of higher precipitation seasonality and lower annual mean temperature was exceptional among exhaustive count-preserving state maps.

The observed finite-map ranks were 16/792 (2.02%) for the n≥5 panel, 19/1,716 (1.11%) for n≥3 and 4/126 (3.17%) under the strict n≥10 occurrence gate. The reverse downward-to-upward direction tracked the opposite side of the same strict present-niche vector, with a bidirectional-floor rank of 3/126 (2.38%).

The upward-to-downward signal remained exceptional after linear latitude/longitude residualization (5/126 = 3.97%), after restricting the score to internal edges (3/126 = 2.38%) and after combining geography residualization with internal-edge-only scoring (3/126 = 2.38%).

Thus, orientation transition direction is non-randomly associated with a composite present ecological regime under the declared phylogenetic reconstruction. The supported object is a niche regime rather than a single climatic-variable effect.

## Reproductive antagonists impose a large fitness cost in *Cirsium*

Across four independent experimental *Cirsium* programmes, reducing reproductive insect herbivory increased viable or mature seed output by a pooled response ratio of **2.674** (95% CI **2.388–2.993**). This corresponds to an estimated **62.6%** reduction in potential viable or mature seed output under ambient herbivory, with a 95% interval of **58.1–66.6%**. Between-study heterogeneity for this narrowly harmonized estimand was low (`I² = 1.0%`).

Leave-one-program-out pooled response ratios remained between 2.60 and 2.73 and every 95% interval remained above one.

The result establishes a large and repeatable antagonist fitness channel in *Cirsium*. It does not by itself identify which capitulum morphology provides protection.

## Functional evidence maps the three traits onto different ecological interfaces

For orientation, the closest Asteraceae manipulation showed that experimentally erect heads produced substantially fewer achenes than naturally nodding heads, while water and UV-B exposure reduced pollen viability. No pollinator preference between manipulated angles and no meaningful orientation-driven internal-temperature difference were detected. Together with the East-Asian transition–niche result, the leading ecological interpretation is that orientation can act as an **abiotic reproductive-exposure filter**, especially through wetting/rain and radiation exposure, while pollination timing remains a secondary route.

For phyllary architecture, removal of involucral spines in *Centaurea solstitialis* reduced deterrence of illegitimate Lepidoptera and reduced filled seed production by 22%, without increasing legitimate bee/fly visit frequency. Within *Cirsium*, antagonist oviposition on the phyllary surface can be associated with large reductions in mature seed production, while naturally appressed *C. sieboldii* still receives bee, butterfly and hawkmoth visitation. The leading ecological interpretation is therefore a **mechanical access filter** that can restrict damaging access without requiring open phyllaries for legitimate visitation.

Stickiness showed a different pattern. Occluding sticky exudates increased seed predation in one *C. discolor* context but had no detectable effect in *C. flodmani*; later *C. discolor* neutralization also produced no reduction in successful seed output. Natural-history observations showed that pollinators did not contact sticky traps, several seed predators bypassed them, ants and aphids were deterred and some predatory arthropods used sticky inflorescences. Sticky involucres are therefore best interpreted as a **guild-selective arthropod-community filter with context-dependent net benefit**, not as a universally effective seed-predator barrier.

The three functional mappings are thus qualitatively different: orientation regulates abiotic exposure, phyllary architecture regulates mechanical access, and stickiness regulates arthropod access and residence in a guild-dependent manner.

## Multi-agent experiments support ecological context dependence rather than one universal selective agent

Across five strict factorial floral-selection programmes, two were antagonist-dominant, one pollinator-dominant and two mixed or trait-dependent. More than one category remained after every leave-one-program-out sensitivity.

This synthesis rejects both universal pollinator dominance and universal antagonist dominance as general descriptions of floral selection. Instead, it supports a selection-mosaic interpretation in which the sign and magnitude of selection on reproductive traits depend on the local balance of mutualists, antagonists and other environmental pressures.

# Discussion

## A complex capitulum can be historically integrated yet ecologically multi-interface

The central result is a positive ecological interpretation of mosaic evolution. Orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness all changed repeatedly within a young radiation, but their changes occupy different relative depths and do not repeatedly localize to the same branches. At the same time, the strongest available functional evidence maps the three traits onto different interfaces between the capitulum and its environment.

This combination provides a biologically coherent explanation for why one reproductive structure can have a mosaic evolutionary history. The capitulum is not exposed to one ecological problem. It must present flowers to mutualists, protect reproductive tissues from abiotic exposure, restrict damaging access to developing seeds and interact with a heterogeneous arthropod community. These pressures need not covary among habitats or lineages. Consequently, selection can reorganize different components of the same head at different points in the radiation while the extant capitulum remains functionally integrated as one reproductive unit.

The result therefore differs from both extreme alternatives. It does not support a single persistent whole-capitulum syndrome whose components repeatedly change together. But it also does not imply complete evolutionary independence of the traits. **Mosaic ecological assembly** is the narrower interpretation: components of one structure can be coupled in present function while carrying partly distinct evolutionary histories because they mediate partly distinct ecological interfaces.

## Orientation provides a direct bridge between reconstructed history and present ecology

Orientation is the strongest focal example because both its evolutionary history and present ecological association are relatively well resolved. The upward-to-downward transition probability tracks a composite regime of greater precipitation seasonality and lower annual temperature, and this result persists after removing simple linear geographic structure and terminal-edge contribution.

The biological meaning of this composite should not be reduced to “BIO15 causes nodding”. BIO15 and BIO1 are ecological coordinates. The closest direct manipulation suggests a more mechanistic interpretation involving exposure of pollen and reproductive tissues to water and UV-B. A downward head can alter whether floral tissues receive direct precipitation and radiation, whereas the absence of a static pollinator preference in the closest manipulation weakens a simple attraction-only explanation.

This generates a specific ecological model: orientation controls an abiotic exposure surface whose reproductive consequences depend on local weather and phenological context. The current East-Asian niche association is compatible with that model and makes the head-angle transition an especially informative target for direct focal-genus manipulation.

## Phyllaries and stickiness reveal two different antagonist interfaces

The strong reproductive cost of insect herbivory in *Cirsium* makes antagonist interactions evolutionarily plausible: experimentally reducing herbivory more than doubled mature or viable seed output across independent programmes. The relevant question is therefore not whether antagonists can matter, but which capitulum traits modify that pathway and under what conditions.

Phyllary architecture and stickiness should not be collapsed into one generic “defence” axis. Structural phyllaries and spines can alter the mechanical route by which visitors approach the reproductive head. The closest Cardueae experiment shows a direct structure-to-access-to-seed pathway, and *Cirsium* enemy studies establish that successful antagonist access can strongly reduce seed production. This makes mechanical access control a biologically credible explanation for repeated phyllary evolution.

Stickiness, by contrast, does not behave as a universal barrier. Direct *Cirsium* manipulations produce benefit and null outcomes, while arthropod guilds differ in their ability or tendency to contact, avoid, bypass or exploit sticky structures. The evolutionary value of stickiness should therefore depend on community composition: the same adhesive trait can deter one guild, fail against another and potentially alter predator or sap-feeder activity. Such context dependence provides a natural ecological explanation for why a trait can be repeatedly gained or lost near the tips of a radiation without requiring one fixed selective advantage.

## Selection mosaic provides a unifying ecological model without requiring one common driver

The three trait histories can be integrated conceptually without forcing them into one causal mechanism. Multi-agent floral-selection experiments show that neither pollinators nor antagonists dominate universally; the dominant contribution varies among systems and traits. The capitulum can therefore be viewed as a set of ecological interfaces operating under a selection mosaic.

Under this model, local fitness consequences arise from the combination of at least three terms:

\[
\text{net selection on a capitulum trait}
=
\text{mutualist benefit}
+
\text{abiotic benefit/cost}
-
\text{antagonist cost}
-
\text{trait-production or access cost}.
\]

The relative weights of these terms differ among environments. A trait such as orientation can primarily change abiotic exposure in one context while also influencing visitor presentation in another. Phyllary posture can reduce antagonist access but potentially alter handling by legitimate visitors. Stickiness can be beneficial, neutral or costly depending on which arthropod guilds dominate. Repeated evolution of different capitulum traits therefore need not be driven by one recurring climate variable or one universal biotic agent.

This ecological model is more informative than treating the negative outcome of any one historical-climate screen as the headline result. The positive result is that repeated capitulum evolution is organized in a way that is consistent with **trait-specific ecological filtering**.

## What the present study establishes—and what remains causal

The current evidence supports four statements. First, multiple capitulum traits repeatedly changed within one young radiation. Second, their histories differ in relative depth and transition localization. Third, orientation transition direction is associated with a specific present ecological regime. Fourth, independent functional evidence identifies distinct, biologically plausible interface mechanisms for orientation, phyllary architecture and stickiness, while experimental *Cirsium* data show that the antagonist pathway has a large reproductive-fitness consequence.

These statements justify an ecological interpretation of the historical mosaic. They do not establish that every reconstructed transition was independently adaptive, nor do they transfer effect sizes from other taxa or populations to the focal East-Asian events. Direct trait manipulation linked to interaction, mechanism and reproductive fitness is required for that stronger inference.

A separate bounded palaeoenvironmental analysis did not identify the present orientation regime as a robust historical origin regime. We treat that result as a claim boundary rather than the centre of the paper: current public data resolve ecological organization and plausible mechanism more strongly than they resolve the exact selective conditions at the time of origin.

# Conclusion

A young East-Asian *Cirsium* radiation repeatedly reassembled the capitulum from components that do not share one synchronized evolutionary history. Orientation, phyllary posture and involucre stickiness changed repeatedly, occupy unequal relative evolutionary depths and do not repeatedly localize transitions to the same branches. Their functional evidence also points to different ecological roles: orientation as an abiotic reproductive-exposure filter, phyllary architecture as a mechanical access filter, and stickiness as a context-dependent arthropod-community filter. Experimental *Cirsium* studies show that reproductive antagonists can impose a large seed-fitness cost, while multi-agent experiments show that no single selective-agent class dominates universally.

We therefore interpret capitulum diversification as **mosaic ecological evolution across distinct interfaces**, rather than repeated evolution of one fixed adaptive syndrome. The main biological claim is not that one climate trigger or one enemy explains the radiation. It is that a complex reproductive structure can be repeatedly reassembled because its component traits mediate different ecological problems whose importance varies among lineages and environments.
