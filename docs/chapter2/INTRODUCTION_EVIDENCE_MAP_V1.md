## Introduction logic

### Paragraph 1 — the general problem

Organisms are multivariate phenotypes. Correlation/integration can constrain or channel variation, but a covariance pattern observed among present phenotypes does not itself specify how traits changed through evolutionary history.

### Paragraph 2 — the missing distinction

Separate three historical quantities:

1. **state conservation** — do relatives retain similar trait values?
2. **recurrence** — how often do defined states change?
3. **change localization** — do large changes in different phenotype dimensions occur on the same branches?

These quantities can disagree. A trait can lack strong present-day phylogenetic signal while changes across multiple traits remain concentrated in the same episodes, or vice versa.

### Paragraph 3 — why a rapid radiation is informative

A shallow radiation compresses large morphological diversity into a short lineage-history interval. It therefore tests whether disparity arose through one conserved syndrome, independent component histories, or coordinated remodeling. *Cirsium* is useful because the Japanese diversity is dominated by a Pleistocene radiation and has extensive public nuclear data.

### Paragraph 4 — what Chapter 1 made possible

Chapter 1 supplied a continuous phenotype ontology from georeferenced photographs rather than forcing species into categorical capitulum types. Chapter 2 reuses that ontology independently of whether a trait had a significant environmental association in Chapter 1 and places it onto the evolutionary tree.

### Paragraph 5 — questions/predictions

Q1. Do continuous phenotype components retain phylogenetic state structure?

Q2. Do independently source-coded discrete traits require repeated transitions?

Q3. Are magnitudes of continuous phenotype change coordinated across branches?

Q4. Is any coordination specifically stronger within the registered present-day phenotype modules?

Q5. Are shared discrete-state transitions robust to topology uncertainty?

The alternatives are not reduced to `syndrome versus modularity`; the analyses allow state conservation and change coordination to differ.
