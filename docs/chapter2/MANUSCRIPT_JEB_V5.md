# Modular evolutionary depth and lineage-dependent environmental correspondence of capitulum traits in a young thistle radiation

**Target journal:** *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* — Research Article

**Manuscript status:** active standalone submission draft v5; rebuilt from the final public-data V3 synthesis

**Running title:** Modular capitulum depth and environment

**Word-limit contract:** main text <=7,500 words; abstract <=250 words; 4–10 keywords

## Abstract

Complex structures may be phenotypically integrated in the present while their constituent traits have different evolutionary histories and environmental correspondences. We combined public phylogenomics, authority-backed trait states, occurrence–climate data, dated East-Asian subclades, public images and a 5-Myr palaeoclimate series to quantify the evolutionary depth of capitulum diversity in *Cirsium*. Orientation required four to six minimum changes, phyllary posture exactly three and involucre stickiness exactly five across a 1,000-topology bootstrap ensemble, but no trait pair retained a robust shared transition-localization pattern. Present ecology was also asymmetric. Downward/nodding orientation occupied higher precipitation-seasonality and lower-temperature niches across all accepted East-Asian topologies, whereas a chronology × palaeolocation envelope for its inferred core-Nipponocirsium origin produced 376 historical scenarios whose state–trajectory cosine spanned −0.799 to +0.609 between the 5th and 95th percentiles, leaving the origin environment unresolved. Two publicly dated white–coloured sister systems independently recovered lower visible corolla chroma in the white lineage, yet the global negative radiation–chroma direction was reproduced by the Arenicola pair and reversed by the Taiwan pair. Both white lineages also showed higher circularity and solidity and lower visible floret fraction, while finer geometry was heterogeneous. Thus, similar capitulum states need not imply a common historical or environmental driver. Public data support modular evolutionary depth with partial coordinated remodelling and lineage- and scale-dependent environmental correspondence, while adaptation and fitness remain untested.

**Keywords:** *Cirsium*; capitulum; environmental correspondence; evolutionary depth; mosaic evolution; palaeoclimate; phylogenetic uncertainty; trait evolution

# Introduction

Complex biological structures are assembled from components that can share development, genetic covariance and function without necessarily sharing one evolutionary history. Morphological integration and modularity are therefore scale-dependent properties rather than mutually exclusive labels, and mosaic evolution can distribute phenotypic change unevenly among components of the same structure (Klingenberg, 2014; Goswami et al., 2014; Felice & Goswami, 2018; Zelditch & Goswami, 2021). A historical analysis should consequently distinguish at least three questions: how often a trait must change, where in a radiation those changes can occur, and whether the environmental context associated with a present phenotype also describes the conditions under which that phenotype arose.

The third question is particularly difficult. Present-day trait–environment sorting is often easier to estimate than historical environmental exposure, but a current association can reflect origin, later maintenance, range sorting, correlated environmental variables or lineage-specific history. Conversely, repeated evolution of a similar phenotype does not guarantee repeated exposure to the same selective agent. Treating present niche differences as ancestral causes, or treating repeated states as evidence of a single adaptive mechanism, can therefore overstate what comparative data identify. The relevant alternatives include a persistent driver that acts both at origin and in current sorting, origin–maintenance decoupling, and driver switching among lineages.

Rapid radiations make these distinctions especially important. Short internodes, incomplete lineage sorting, introgression and incomplete trait coverage can leave multiple ancestral reconstructions compatible with present states. Phylogenetic uncertainty can bias estimated transition counts (Bollback, 2006; Duchêne & Lanfear, 2015), while species-level state coding can compress polymorphism and population structure. Even a stable minimum-change count can coexist with poorly localized events, and even a robust present ecological direction can fail to identify the historical environment of a transition.

The thistle genus *Cirsium* provides a useful system for separating these layers. A broad nuclear phylogenomic analysis recovered rapid Pleistocene radiations, including a large Japanese radiation with substantial gene-tree discordance (Moreyra et al., 2025). Independent East-Asian phylotranscriptomic studies provide local divergence-time constraints and show additional cytological, reticulate and taxonomic complexity (Chang et al., 2025, 2026). The capitulum itself contains separable traits including head orientation, phyllary posture, involucre stickiness, flower colour and continuous head geometry. These modules coexist in one present structure but differ sharply in public-data coverage and in how directly they can be linked to phylogeny, environment and time.

Here we use public data to ask four nested questions. First, **how deep is capitulum diversity**, measured by minimum state-change burden and relative lineage-depth? Second, **do constituent traits share one transition history**, or are their changes localized differently? Third, **how far do present environmental correspondences agree across global, East-Asian, among-lineage and within-lineage scales?** Fourth, where public dates and palaeoenvironmental series permit it, **does the historical environmental trajectory around a transition point in the same direction as present spatial sorting?** We treat unresolved and not-evaluable comparisons as explicit results. The objective is not to infer adaptation from heterogeneous public evidence, but to identify which explanatory links can be closed before direct sampling, mechanism and reproductive-fitness tests.

# Materials and methods

## Study design and evidence hierarchy

We separated evidence into distinct links rather than combining significance values. Repeated history asked whether a phenotype required multiple changes on the admitted topology ensemble. Temporal geometry asked whether those changes were internal, terminal or bounded by public calendar dates. Present spatial evidence asked whether a homologous phenotype was sorted along a frozen environmental gradient. Current ecological correspondence tested whether focal evolutionary states occupied different present niches after phylogenetic correction. Historical environmental correspondence required a dated transition window to align with a predeclared environmental trajectory against matched background windows. Direct mechanism and reproductive fitness were outside the public-data scope.

This hierarchy prevents one evidence type from compensating for another. Minimum changes were not labelled independent origins; shared or non-shared transition localization was not labelled genetic modularity; current environmental correspondence was not labelled historical causation; and dated lineage splits were not treated as exact trait-transition times. `Not_evaluable` denotes insufficient homologous data and is not equivalent to no biological relationship.

## Nuclear scaffold, taxon admission and discrete trait histories

The focal Japanese units were paper taxon concepts linked to Moreyra et al. (2025). We used an independently reconstructed Compositae1061-compatible nuclear phylogram rather than digitizing a published figure. A frozen 241-locus starting set yielded 236 quality-controlled loci, 176 of which were rootable with the safflower outgroup; the concatenated alignment contained 161,654 bp. Maximum-likelihood analysis used IQ-TREE 2, ModelFinder, 1,000 ultrafast-bootstrap replicates and 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017; Hoang et al., 2018; Minh et al., 2020). The tree is treated as a harmonized common-locus Japan38 scaffold, not as the only nuclear evidence for East-Asian *Cirsium*.

Branch lengths are substitutions per site and were not converted to absolute time. Two biological samples assigned to JPN20 were non-monophyletic on the maximum-likelihood tree and all 1,000 bootstrap trees and were not forcibly collapsed. JPN31 had a frozen Japan–Ukraine identity conflict and was excluded from primary trait history. A separate local-topology sensitivity retained six AU-nonrejected optimized arrangements for ecological analyses.

Orientation, phyllary posture and involucre stickiness were defined by separate biological ontologies. States were admitted only when an exact paper concept could be matched to an authority-quality description; ambiguous concepts remained missing and continuous image values were not discretized to enlarge historical coverage. Final historical coverage was 20 concepts for orientation, ten for phyllary posture and 13 for stickiness.

## Minimum-change counts, relative lineage-depth and shared localization

For the maximum-likelihood tree and each of 1,000 raw bootstrap topologies, we calculated the unordered-parsimony minimum for each trait. Dynamic programming then obtained the exact minimum and maximum mean relative lineage-depth across all globally minimum-cost Sankoff histories. For a tree with *N* admitted concept tips and an edge subtending *d* descendants, relative lineage-depth was (*N*−*d*)/(*N*−1); a terminal edge equals one and lower values denote edges subtending broader lineages. This coordinate is topology only and is not an event age or rate.

We also recorded whether a named parent–child edge was forced to change across every globally minimum ancestral assignment and summarized its bootstrap frequency. To test a simple common-history model, an equal-rates Mk diagnostic estimated branch-wise transition probability and excess over the branch prior for each trait. Spearman correlations compared orientation–phyllary, orientation–stickiness and phyllary–stickiness localization. A topology-only sensitivity set all non-root branches to one; a pair passed the descriptive shared-localization rule only when the branch-aware excess correlation was positive and the fifth percentile of the equal-branch bootstrap distribution was also positive.

## Present environmental state space

We used two complementary present-day layers. First, a separately frozen global public-image analysis supplied external state-space predictions without screening new predictors in the focal East-Asian data. The two relevant among-taxon results were a positive association between image-derived capitulum orientation angle and CHELSA annual precipitation (BIO12; standardized beta=0.304359, family-adjusted q=0.021) and a negative association between visible corolla CIELAB chroma and CHELSA mean surface downwelling shortwave radiation (RSDS; beta=−0.345372, q=0.006). Both directions survived their prespecified broad-space and historical-placement sensitivities.

Second, current East-Asian ecology was estimated from source-name-guarded, spatially thinned occurrence data with complete CHELSA extraction. The primary orientation panel contained nine taxa, five upward/erect and four downward/nodding. Across each of six AU-nonrejected optimized phylogenies, generalized least squares with a Brownian shared-path covariance estimated standardized downward-minus-upward effects for annual mean temperature (BIO1), temperature seasonality (BIO4), annual precipitation (BIO12) and precipitation seasonality (BIO15). Species leave-one-out refits produced 54 evaluations per climate axis. Independent Taiwan occurrence-source tiers were retained as sensitivity analyses rather than replacing the frozen GBIF primary after observing their P values.

Phyllary posture and stickiness were evaluated under the same occurrence gate only when at least two state classes remained. Their lack of state-diverse overlap was retained as `not_evaluable` rather than a zero effect.

## Public chronology and palaeolocation envelope for orientation

The Japan38 phylogram itself is undated, so we did not scale relative lineage-depth using the published age of the Japanese radiation. Instead, we used public East-Asian topology and dating information to bound one interpretable orientation event. Public taxon expansion places erect *C. morii* basal to a core Nipponocirsium group in which the sampled Japanese and Taiwan core taxa are nodding. The parsimonious erect-to-nodding change therefore lies after the *C. morii* split and before the Japanese-core/Taiwan-core split, rather than uniquely on the previously restricted Taiwan-trio stem.

The parent and child bounds come from separate public analyses and were not treated as a joint posterior. The parent/*C. morii* split has a central estimate of 0.79 Ma and a marginal interval of 0.43–1.18 Ma; the Japanese-core/Taiwan-core split has a central estimate of 0.74 Ma and an interval of 0.60–0.87 Ma (Chang et al., 2025, 2026). We enumerated a deterministic grid across these marginal intervals, retained only parent-younger-than-child-consistent topology pairs with parent age greater than child age by at least 10 kyr, and retained the central pair. This yielded 94 admissible chronology scenarios with durations from 10 to 580 kyr.

Because the pre-split ancestral area is unresolved, each chronology was evaluated in four predeclared regional scenarios: Taiwan, a Ryukyu corridor, southern Japan and a broader East-Asian core corridor. We extracted BIO1, BIO4, BIO12 and BIO15 from PALEO-PGEM-Series, which provides 1° global estimates every 1 kyr through the last 5 Myr (Barreto et al., 2023). For each region and duration, historical young-minus-old environmental change was standardized against all same-duration windows from the available regional time series. The four-dimensional historical trajectory was compared with the frozen global orientation state-space vector (BIO1=−0.0302, BIO4=−0.1178, BIO12=+0.3044, BIO15=+0.0670) using cosine similarity. The matched-window percentile of each cosine quantified whether alignment was unusual for that duration and region.

A robust historical concordance required the same positive direction across chronology and palaeolocation uncertainty and unusually high matched-window alignment. Robust discordance required the opposite. Otherwise the origin trajectory was classified as unresolved. The previously analysed 0.79–0.47 Ma Taiwan descendant interval was retained only as a restricted sensitivity and was not interpreted as the unique origin event.

## Public-image natural experiments for flower colour and head geometry

The Japan38 sequence panel does not provide a one-to-one species-tip flower-colour history because extant colour polymorphism and morph–sample mismatch are common. We therefore used two public, independently dated white–coloured sister comparisons as focused natural experiments rather than manufacturing a full discrete colour reconstruction: *C. brevicaule* (white) versus *C. irumtiense* (bluish-purple) in Arenicola, with a published lineage-divergence context near 0.93 Ma, and *C. kawakamii* (white) versus *C. tatakaense* (purple) in Taiwan, with a context near 0.35 Ma (Chang et al., 2026). Split ages provide lineage context, not exact flower-colour transition times.

Public iNaturalist observations were filtered under one source and image-quality contract. Duplicate public photo IDs were removed. Candidate observations and photographs were selected before phenotype measurement, and trait-neutral multiscale head crops were chosen only by the frozen foreground-mask quality, sharpness and resolution. The same image-measurement implementation used in the global public-image atlas was pinned without changing its trait thresholds. After the neutral quality gate, each taxon was balanced to 14 observations.

The preregistered assay gate required the white lineage to have lower Azami-compatible corolla Lab chroma in both sister systems with at least three usable colour observations per role. Only after that gate passed did we inspect prespecified non-colour endpoints. Same-direction extant contrasts were treated as hypothesis-generating repeated phenotype directions, not reconstructed simultaneous transitions, developmental coupling or correlated evolution.

## Focal current RSDS concordance in the two colour systems

We next tested one frozen environmental prediction only: higher current CHELSA RSDS should correspond to lower visible chroma. No environmental-variable screen was performed. RSDS was extracted at all 56 balanced observation coordinates using the same CHELSA v2.1 source and sampling implementation as the global analysis (Karger et al., 2017).

For each sister system, the primary estimand was the white-minus-coloured difference in taxon-median RSDS using all 14 balanced coordinates and the white-minus-coloured difference in median chroma among usable colour observations. Concordance with the global direction required RSDS>0 and chroma<0. A prespecified locality sensitivity collapsed observations to taxon × 0.05° cells. As a secondary scale diagnostic, chroma and RSDS were demeaned within taxon and a pooled standardized slope was tested by permutation restricted within taxa. This within-taxon result could diagnose scale dependence but could not override the sister-pair primary estimand.

## Integrated whole-capitulum synthesis

The final synthesis combined seven trait × driver rows rather than a numerical evidence score. Historical recurrence, temporal geometry, present spatial sorting, current ecology, mechanism priors, historical environment and focal fitness represent non-exchangeable links. We also retained two independent constraints on a one-syndrome model: zero of three discrete trait pairs passed the robust shared-transition-localization rule, and a separate 18-dimensional present-day complete-case analysis yielded only partial similarity between within- and among-taxon association matrices (Spearman rho=0.3663).

The two colour sister systems supplied an additional test of partial coordinated remodelling. Shared directions in coarse head geometry were retained only when the same metric pointed in the same direction in both comparisons. Heterogeneous or low-information detailed geometry remained explicit negative evidence against a universal whole-capitulum syndrome.

# Results

## Capitulum traits show unequal but repeated evolutionary depth

Published phylogenomic evidence placed 36 of 38 sampled Japanese concepts within the dominant Japanese radiation. Within the authority-backed subset, multiple orientation × stickiness configurations occurred inside that radiation, showing that present capitulum configuration did not map one-to-one onto broad colonization history.

All three completed discrete trait histories required multiple changes. Orientation required six changes on the maximum-likelihood tree and four to six across 1,000 bootstrap topologies, with a median of five. Phyllary posture required exactly three changes on the maximum-likelihood tree and every bootstrap topology. Stickiness required exactly five changes on all corresponding trees.

The depth of admissible minimum histories differed markedly. On the maximum-likelihood topology, exact mean relative lineage-depth envelopes were 0.767–1.000 for orientation, 0.695–1.000 for phyllary posture and 0.943–0.954 for stickiness. Across bootstrap topologies, median lower–upper envelopes were 0.795–0.994, 0.695–1.000 and 0.937–0.954, respectively. Stickiness therefore combined a stable five-change burden with a particularly shallow and narrow admissible depth range, whereas phyllary posture allowed much broader placements.

Named-edge localization did not reduce these histories to one set of obvious events. No orientation edge was individually forced on the maximum-likelihood tree; JPN36 was forced in 0.227 of bootstrap topologies. The JPN36 terminal phyllary edge was forced in 0.728. For stickiness, JPN06, JPN36 and JPN30 terminal edges were forced in 0.995, 0.707 and 0.545 of topologies, and one nine-tip internal edge in 0.681. Stable minimum counts, temporal depth and edge localization were therefore distinct properties of the histories.

## A simple shared transition history is not supported

None of the three discrete trait pairs passed the prespecified cross-treatment rule for a common transition-localization pattern. On the branch-length-aware maximum-likelihood tree, transition-excess correlations were positive but modest for orientation–phyllary, orientation–stickiness and phyllary–stickiness; the equal-branch bootstrap distributions did not retain a positive fifth percentile for any pair. Thus, the present capitulum does not behave as if orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness were repeatedly reconfigured on the same branches.

This result constrains a universal common-lability model but does not imply complete independence. The traits differ in coverage and may still share developmental or ecological covariance that is not expressed as identical branch localization.

## Orientation has strong present hydric correspondence but an unresolved origin environment

Current East-Asian orientation ecology was directionally stable. Across all six accepted topologies, downward/nodding taxa occupied niches with higher precipitation seasonality: standardized BIO15 downward-minus-upward effects ranged from +1.320 to +1.330. They also occupied lower annual mean temperature niches, with BIO1 effects from −0.975 to −0.967. Both signs were retained in 54/54 topology × species leave-one-out fits. The GBIF primary remained threshold-unresolved, whereas one direct Taiwan source tier crossed the frozen tendency threshold; the biological directions did not depend on source tier.

The independent global spatial result pointed to the same broad hydric domain through a different environmental statistic: among taxa, larger orientation angle was associated with greater annual precipitation BIO12 (beta=+0.304359, q=0.021). BIO12 and BIO15 are not the same variable, so this constitutes hydric-domain correspondence rather than replication of one coefficient.

Historical analysis did not close the causal gap. The core-Nipponocirsium origin envelope contained 94 admissible age pairs across four palaeolocation scenarios, for 376 region × chronology cases. State–trajectory cosine similarity had a 5th percentile of −0.799, median −0.065 and 95th percentile +0.609; the median matched-window percentile was 0.470. The historical trajectory therefore changed sign and tail status across admissible chronology and location scenarios and was classified as `origin_trajectory_unresolved_under_public_chronology_and_paleolocation_uncertainty`.

The restricted Taiwan descendant sensitivity reinforced why present sorting cannot simply be read backward as origin. Its annual precipitation change was not exceptional, precipitation seasonality changed opposite the present downward-state association, and annual mean temperature warmed rather than shifted toward the present cooler downward-state niche. These results do not refute hydric effects on current sorting or fitness; they show that the public data do not identify hydric change as the historical origin driver.

## Two dated white lineages repeat the colour phenotype but not one current RSDS context

The public-image assay gate passed in both dated sister systems. In Arenicola, white *C. brevicaule* had lower median visible chroma than coloured *C. irumtiense* by −2.95 units and higher lightness by +6.86. In Taiwan, white *C. kawakamii* had lower chroma than *C. tatakaense* by −6.16 and higher lightness by +8.24. Bootstrap intervals for chroma were wide and included zero in each small colour-usable subset, so the result is replicated direction plus assay validation rather than two independently significant effects.

Current radiation context differed between the two systems. In Arenicola, the white-minus-coloured RSDS contrast was +1814 stored raster units, while chroma was lower, reproducing the frozen global higher-RSDS/lower-chroma direction. After 0.05° locality aggregation the RSDS contrast remained +1712. In Taiwan, however, the white lineage had *lower* current RSDS than its coloured sister: the white-minus-coloured contrast was −686.5, with a bootstrap interval entirely below zero, and became −1703 after locality aggregation. The white phenotype was therefore repeated under opposite pair-level RSDS contrasts.

At a different biological scale, the secondary pooled within-taxon analysis returned a negative standardized chroma–RSDS slope (beta=−0.4065). Its two-sided permutation P was 0.1141 and the prespecified expected-negative one-sided P was 0.0361. This secondary result does not overturn the 1/2 pair-level primary outcome. Instead, it provides a direct example of hierarchical scale dependence: global among-taxon, sister-lineage and within-taxon environmental relationships need not be identical.

## Repeated white states include partial coarse head remodelling, not a universal syndrome

After the colour assay gate passed, three coarse non-colour image endpoints showed the same direction in both sister comparisons. White lineages had higher circularity (+0.238 in Arenicola; +0.159 in Taiwan), higher solidity (+0.092; +0.099) and lower visible floret fraction (−0.305; −0.028). Only the Arenicola circularity and solidity bootstrap intervals excluded zero, so the replicated result is directional rather than uniformly strong.

Fine geometry did not show the same consistency. Aspect ratio and width-profile variation pointed in opposite directions across systems, while detailed involucre projection, taper and spread metrics were heterogeneous or had too few usable observations. The white state is therefore associated with a repeatable coarse packing/display signal in these two comparisons, but not with a universal white-flower whole-capitulum syndrome.

## The final public-data model is modular and hierarchically scale dependent

Across the complete synthesis, orientation had the strongest historical-depth and present-environment bridge, but its origin environment remained unresolved. Flower colour showed a repeated extant white-state phenotype in two dated lineage comparisons, but current RSDS correspondence was lineage dependent and historical radiation was not directly recoverable. Phyllary posture retained a strong three-change history without a homologous environmental bridge. Stickiness retained a shallow five-change history while a universal generic-defence explanation remained unsupported by the current comparative evidence.

At the whole-capitulum level, zero of three discrete trait pairs shared a robust transition-localization pattern, yet the two white lineage systems repeated three coarse non-colour directions and the independent 18-dimensional present analysis showed partial within-versus-among association similarity (rho=0.3663). The data therefore support neither complete trait independence nor one synchronized syndrome. The best current public-data description is a **modular hierarchical mosaic with partial coordinated remodelling**: capitulum components differ in evolutionary depth, and environmental correspondence can differ across traits, lineages and biological scales.

# Discussion

## Evolutionary depth is more than a transition count

The capitulum histories show why diversity depth cannot be reduced to one ancestral-state reconstruction. Orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness all require repeated change, but their count stability, admissible lineage depth and edge localization differ. Stickiness is especially informative: five changes are invariant across the topology ensemble, yet their admissible history is much shallower and more tightly bounded than the phyllary history. Conversely, orientation combines a variable four-to-six change burden with weak named-edge concentration.

These differences are biologically meaningful even without exact event dates. They show that the present capitulum was assembled through trait-specific histories rather than one synchronized sequence. At the same time, minimum changes are lower bounds and do not distinguish independent origins from reversals, sorting of ancestral variation or introgression. The result is therefore repeated historical reassembly, not demonstrated convergence.

## Present environmental sorting does not identify historical origin

Orientation provides the strongest example of the distinction between current correspondence and origin. Global annual precipitation sorting and East-Asian precipitation-seasonality differences independently point to a hydric domain, and the East-Asian direction is exceptionally stable to topology choice and taxon deletion. This makes hydric exposure a strong candidate domain for current orientation ecology.

Yet the dated origin-envelope analysis fails the stronger historical test. Once uncertainty in both chronology and ancestral region is propagated, the historical environmental trajectory can align positively or negatively with the present state-space direction. The matched-window distribution is also unexceptional on average. Present ecological sorting is therefore better resolved than the environmental context of origin.

This result is not a failure of the hydric hypothesis. It separates three processes that are often conflated: a factor can contribute to trait origin, maintain or sort a trait after origin, or become correlated with the trait during later range evolution. With the current public data, persistent driver, origin–maintenance decoupling and driver switching remain distinguishable hypotheses rather than interchangeable interpretations.

## The same white phenotype need not imply the same radiative driver

The colour natural experiment makes this problem visible at a second trait. The two dated sister comparisons independently recover the expected white-state colour phenotype using one frozen measurement pipeline. If the global negative RSDS–chroma association represented a universal current environmental rule for repeated white states, both sister systems should point in the same pair-level direction. They do not. Arenicola is concordant with the global association, whereas the Taiwan contrast is reversed and remains reversed after locality aggregation.

The negative pooled within-taxon slope adds an important scale contrast rather than rescuing a universal rule. Environmental effects can differ between local within-lineage responses and among-lineage state differences because geography, elevation, ancestry and correlated climate covary differently at each scale. The public result therefore weakens a universal persistent-RSDS model for white states while leaving several alternatives open: the same pigment phenotype may arise under different selective environments; current maintenance may differ from origin; or the present RSDS contrast may proxy different environmental packages in different lineages.

Historical radiation cannot currently adjudicate these alternatives. PALEO-PGEM supplies temperature and precipitation rather than a directly commensurate surface-RSDS history, and the public lineage splits do not date exact colour transitions. Treating orbital forcing or another radiation model as direct replication would introduce a new model layer. The appropriate conclusion is therefore lineage- and scale-dependent current correspondence with historical radiative causation unresolved.

## Partial coordinated remodelling changes the whole-capitulum interpretation

The white sister systems also prevent the opposite overcorrection—that every capitulum trait evolves independently. Circularity, solidity and visible floret fraction shift in the same direction in both white lineage comparisons. This repeated coarse geometry could reflect shared development, correlated selection, packing constraints, display consequences or an image-level geometric consequence of the floral state. The current public data cannot distinguish among those mechanisms.

At finer scales, however, geometry is heterogeneous, and the discrete orientation, phyllary and stickiness histories do not share one robust localization pattern. The whole capitulum therefore occupies an intermediate position: partial module covariance is real enough to generate testable hypotheses, but neither synchronized evolutionary history nor a common developmental programme is demonstrated.

This intermediate model is more informative than choosing between integration and modularity as binary alternatives. It predicts that some trait combinations should recur because of shared constraints or functions, while others should respond to lineage-specific ecological opportunities. It also predicts that the same coarse phenotype could be produced by different genetic or developmental routes—a question that public photographs and species-level tips cannot resolve.

## Public data define the next causal experiments

The final synthesis is useful because it ranks both convergence and discordance. Orientation × hydric exposure is the first experimental priority because repeated history and current hydric correspondence are independently strong, whereas the missing links are direct flower wetting, pollen performance, effective pollinator contact and viable seed. A decisive test therefore requires gravity-calibrated orientation, rain exposure or shielding manipulations, sham controls and reproductive-fitness endpoints on ancestry-resolved populations.

The Arenicola–Taiwan colour contrast is valuable for a different reason. It deliberately pairs similar white-state phenotypes with opposite current pair-level RSDS contrasts. Morph-linked genomic sampling, calibrated visible and UV reflectance, pigment chemistry, floral temperature or photodamage, pollinator perception and irradiance manipulation can ask whether the phenotypic mechanism is shared even if the ecological context differs, or whether both mechanism and driver have switched.

Phyllary posture and stickiness require still different data. Their repeated histories are stronger than their current driver identification. Direct botanical posture measurements, rain entry, enemy access, pollinator access, secretion neutralization/restoration, production cost and filled-achene output are more informative than extending coarse climate correlations. These tests follow from the public-data gaps rather than from selecting systems that already support an adaptive narrative.

## Limits of the public-data synthesis

Several boundaries remain important. The full Japan38 history is a substitutions-per-site phylogram, not a chronogram. The core-Nipponocirsium age envelope combines marginal intervals from separate studies and is not a joint posterior. Palaeolocation scenarios are regional alternatives, not ancestral-area probabilities. PALEO-PGEM has 1° spatial resolution and does not reconstruct fine mountain microclimate, topography or land–sea geometry dynamically at the scale required for local exposure. Present RSDS is an observational gradient rather than a direct measure of historical solar selection. Public photographs contain residual sampling and image biases even after trait-neutral balancing.

Most importantly, none of the analyses measures reproductive fitness. The terms `persistent driver`, `origin–maintenance decoupling` and `selection mosaic` are explanatory process models to be tested, not demonstrated selective histories. The present contribution is to show exactly where public evidence converges, where it changes direction, and where the next claim becomes non-identifiable without new samples.

# Conclusion

Capitulum diversity in the young Japanese and East-Asian *Cirsium* radiation has unequal evolutionary depth across component traits. Orientation requires four to six minimum changes, phyllary posture three and stickiness five, and no discrete trait pair shares a robust transition-localization pattern. Orientation shows the strongest present hydric correspondence, but a 376-scenario chronology × palaeolocation analysis leaves its historical origin environment unresolved. Two dated white lineages independently reproduce lower visible chroma and partial coarse head remodelling, yet their current RSDS contrasts point in opposite directions. Similar phenotypes therefore need not imply identical environmental histories, and present trait covariance does not imply one synchronized evolutionary trajectory. The most defensible public-data model is modular and hierarchically scale dependent, with partial coordinated remodelling nested within trait-specific histories. Direct exposure, mechanism and reproductive fitness remain the boundary between this comparative synthesis and an adaptive explanation.
