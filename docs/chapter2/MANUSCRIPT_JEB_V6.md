# Repeated capitulum differentiation at unequal evolutionary depths in a young East-Asian thistle radiation

**Target journal:** *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* — Research Article  
**Manuscript status:** V6 scientific draft; historical-differentiation reframe  
**Running title:** Capitulum differentiation through time

## Abstract

How phenotypic differences originate during rapid radiations is often less identifiable than the histories that produced them. We combined public nuclear phylogenomics, authority-backed capitulum states, dated East-Asian subclades, a 5-Myr palaeoclimate series and two independent global sea-level reconstructions to separate repeated trait differentiation from its historical environmental context in *Cirsium*. Orientation required four to six minimum changes across 1,000 bootstrap topologies, phyllary posture exactly three and involucre stickiness exactly five. Their relative-depth profiles differed: phyllary changes could occur on deeper lineages, orientation spanned internal-to-terminal histories, and stickiness was strongly shallow-biased. No discrete trait pair retained robust shared transition localization. Calendar-time identifiability was much weaker than recurrence: only one orientation transition could be bounded by both published node ages and alternative palaeolocation scenarios. A central 0.79–0.74 Ma chronology suggested coherent cooling and seasonality changes, but no BIO1, BIO4, BIO12 or BIO15 direction, environmental level, absolute change or temporal variability survived the full 94-chronology × four-region uncertainty envelope. An independent 5.3-Myr sea-level reconstruction likewise yielded no robust event-associated metric across all chronologies. Thus, capitulum modules repeatedly differentiated at unequal evolutionary depths, whereas a common Pleistocene environmental trigger remains unidentified. These results distinguish a well-resolved history of phenotypic assembly from the more weakly identifiable causes of individual transitions.

**Keywords:** *Cirsium*; capitulum; evolutionary depth; historical environment; palaeoclimate; phenotypic differentiation; phylogenetic uncertainty; rapid radiation

# Introduction

Complex structures evolve through changes in components that may share development and function without sharing identical histories. Morphological integration and modularity are therefore not mutually exclusive states: the same structure can contain components that covary in the present yet change at different rates, on different branches and under different ecological contexts (Klingenberg, 2014; Goswami et al., 2014; Felice & Goswami, 2018; Zelditch & Goswami, 2021). In rapidly diversifying lineages, this raises a basic historical question. Is phenotypic diversity assembled by repeated changes concentrated at a common evolutionary depth, or do different components differentiate at different stages of a radiation?

Answering that question requires more than counting transitions. A minimum-change reconstruction identifies how many changes are required by observed tip states and a given topology, but it does not identify independent origins, exact transition branches or calendar ages. Short internodes, incomplete lineage sorting, introgression and incomplete state coverage can leave many ancestral assignments compatible with the same minimum cost (Bollback, 2006; Duchêne & Lanfear, 2015). Two traits can therefore have similar transition counts but very different admissible temporal geometries. Conversely, a trait with a stable count across topologies can remain poorly localized in calendar time.

The harder question is what environmental conditions accompanied differentiation. Extant distributions are not direct observations of ancestral exposure, and a dated lineage split is not automatically the date of a phenotypic transition. Historical environmental tests require a defensible chain from a trait transition to a bounded branch, a calendar interval, a plausible ancestral location and an environmental time series. Even then, a visually compelling trajectory at one central age can disappear when node-age and palaeogeographic uncertainty are propagated. Distinguishing a lineage-level Pleistocene diversification context from a trait-specific historical trigger is therefore essential before invoking natural selection or adaptation.

The thistle genus *Cirsium* is well suited to this distinction. Global phylogenomics recovered rapid Pleistocene radiations, including a species-rich Japanese radiation with substantial gene-tree discordance (Moreyra et al., 2025). Independent East-Asian phylotranscriptomic studies provide divergence-time constraints for Japanese, Ryukyu and Taiwanese lineages while documenting reticulation, chromosome evolution and recent island differentiation (Chang et al., 2025, 2026). The capitulum combines several biologically distinct traits, including head orientation, phyllary posture, involucre stickiness, flower colour and display geometry. These traits coexist in one reproductive structure but differ greatly in the quality of public historical evidence available for them.

Here we ask four nested questions. First, how repeatedly and how deeply did capitulum traits differentiate within the young radiation? Second, do orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness share a common transition-localization history? Third, which phenotypic transitions can actually be bounded in calendar time rather than merely assigned a relative phylogenetic depth? Fourth, for the best-bounded event, does any historical climate or sea-level signal remain unusual after chronology and palaeolocation uncertainty are propagated? We treat non-identifiability as an explicit result rather than replacing missing historical information with present distributions or dated lineage splits. Our objective is to distinguish the history of phenotypic differentiation from the stronger causal claim that a particular environmental change generated that differentiation.

# Materials and methods

## Study design and evidence hierarchy

We separated historical evidence into sequential links. **Recurrence** asked how many unordered state changes were minimally required. **Relative depth** asked whether globally minimum histories placed those changes on broad internal or shallow terminal lineages. **Calendar identifiability** asked whether a change-bearing lineage could be bounded by published node ages without deriving dates from branch lengths measured in substitutions per site. **Palaeolocation** retained alternative ancestral-region scenarios when geographic history was unresolved. **Historical environmental context** then measured environmental level, signed change, absolute change and within-window variability against same-duration background windows. A **repeated historical trigger** was considered evaluable only if at least two independently bounded homologous trait transitions survived their own chronology and palaeolocation uncertainty.

These links were not treated as exchangeable evidence. Minimum changes were not labelled independent origins or convergence. Relative lineage depth was not converted into millions of years. Published split or dispersal ages were not assigned to phenotypic transitions unless the change-bearing branch was explicitly reconciled. A lineage-level Pleistocene diversification context could not substitute for an event-level environmental test. Selection and reproductive fitness were not measured.

## Nuclear scaffold and trait-state admission

The focal Japanese units were paper taxon concepts linked to the broad phylogenomic sampling of Moreyra et al. (2025). We used a harmonized Compositae1061-compatible nuclear phylogram reconstructed independently from public data rather than digitizing a published figure. A frozen 241-locus starting set yielded 236 quality-controlled loci, of which 176 were rootable with the safflower outgroup; the concatenated alignment contained 161,654 bp. Maximum-likelihood analysis used IQ-TREE 2 with ModelFinder, 1,000 ultrafast-bootstrap replicates and 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017; Hoang et al., 2018; Minh et al., 2020). Branch lengths are substitutions per site and were never interpreted as time.

Orientation, phyllary posture and involucre stickiness were defined under separate biological ontologies. States entered the historical analysis only when an exact paper concept could be matched to authority-quality botanical descriptions. Ambiguous concepts remained missing, and continuous image values were not discretized to enlarge the historical sample. Final coverage was 20 concepts for orientation, 10 for phyllary posture and 13 for stickiness. Known taxonomic/sample conflicts were retained as explicit exclusions or sensitivity boundaries rather than forcibly reconciled.

## Minimum changes and relative lineage depth

For the maximum-likelihood tree and each of 1,000 raw bootstrap topologies, we calculated the unordered-parsimony minimum for each trait. Dynamic programming then enumerated the extrema of mean relative lineage depth across all globally minimum-cost Sankoff histories. For a tree with *N* admitted concept tips and an edge subtending *d* descendants, relative lineage depth was (*N*−*d*)/(*N*−1). Terminal edges equal one; lower values denote edges subtending broader descendant sets. This is a topology coordinate, not a calendar age.

We additionally recorded minimum numbers of internal and terminal changes and whether named parent–child edges were forced to change across every globally minimum ancestral assignment. Bootstrap distributions were retained rather than replacing the topology ensemble with a single reconstruction.

## Shared transition localization

To test the simplest synchronized-history model, an equal-rates Mk diagnostic estimated branch-wise transition probability and excess over the branch prior for orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness. Spearman correlations compared pairwise transition localization. A topology-only sensitivity set all non-root branches to equal length. A trait pair passed the descriptive shared-localization rule only when the branch-aware excess correlation was positive and the fifth percentile of the equal-branch bootstrap distribution was also positive. Failure of this rule was interpreted as a boundary on synchronized history, not proof of genetic independence.

## Calendar-event identifiability audit

We built a trait-event ledger before inspecting palaeoenvironmental results. Public dated results were separated into four classes: (1) trait transitions with defensible calendar and palaeolocation envelopes; (2) conditional dated terminal branch envelopes whose ancestral trait state or transition position remained uncertain; (3) dated sister-lineage phenotype contrasts that were not reconstructed transitions; and (4) dated range processes whose ages were not trait-transition ages.

The global Japanese phylogram itself is undated. We therefore conducted a separate recovery audit for machine-readable dated trees or posterior tree sets associated with Moreyra et al. (2025) and the East-Asian studies of Chang et al. (2025, 2026). Public journal routes provided raw sequence archives and supplementary documents but no reusable dated Newick or posterior tree set that could be reconciled to all admitted Japan38 concepts. We consequently did not infer dates from published tree graphics and did not scale relative lineage depth by the approximate age of the Japanese radiation.

## Orientation chronology and palaeolocation envelope

One orientation event could be bounded using independent East-Asian topology and dating information. Erect *C. morii* is basal to a core *Nipponocirsium* group in which sampled Japanese and Taiwanese core taxa are nodding. The parsimonious erect/upward-to-nodding/downward transition therefore lies after the *C. morii* split and before the Japanese-core/Taiwan-core split.

The two node ages come from separate analyses and were not treated as a joint posterior. The parent split has a central estimate of 0.79 Ma and a marginal interval of 0.43–1.18 Ma; the child split has a central estimate of 0.74 Ma and an interval of 0.60–0.87 Ma (Chang et al., 2025, 2026). We enumerated deterministic grids over both marginal intervals, retained only parent ages older than child ages by at least 10 kyr and added the central pair. This produced 94 admissible chronology pairs with durations of 10–580 kyr.

Because ancestral location before the Japanese–Taiwan split is unresolved, each chronology was evaluated under four predeclared regional scenarios: Taiwan, a Ryukyu corridor, southern Japan and a broader East-Asian core corridor. These boxes are sensitivity scenarios, not reconstructed ancestral-area probabilities.

## Historical climate: direction, level, magnitude and variability

We extracted annual mean temperature (BIO1), temperature seasonality (BIO4), annual precipitation (BIO12) and precipitation seasonality (BIO15) from PALEO-PGEM-Series, a 1° global climate time series at 1-kyr resolution across the last 5 Myr (Barreto et al., 2023). For each chronology × region combination we calculated younger-minus-older endpoint change. We then compared the event window with all same-duration windows stepped through the same regional time series.

Four event-level estimands were retained separately: (i) mean environmental level within the event window, (ii) signed endpoint change, (iii) absolute endpoint change and (iv) temporal standard deviation within the window. Percentile positions within matched same-duration windows were descriptive scenario statistics, not posterior probabilities. A variable was considered robustly high or low only if its uncertainty envelope remained in the corresponding extreme tail across the predeclared chronology and palaeolocation scenarios. Central estimates were reported for orientation but never allowed to override the full uncertainty envelope.

## Global sea-level sensitivity

We used global sea level only as a range-reorganization context variable, not as a selective pressure on capitulum orientation. Two independent reconstructions were evaluated.

First, the Spratt–Lisiecki Late Pleistocene stack provides an empirical global reconstruction extending to approximately 0.8 Ma (Spratt & Lisiecki, 2016). Because several admissible orientation parent ages are older, we explicitly recorded chronology coverage before inference. For covered windows we calculated mean sea level, temporal SD, range, absolute endpoint change, mean absolute 1-kyr change and maximum absolute 1-kyr change relative to same-duration background windows.

Second, to obtain complete chronology coverage we used the model-based de Boer Plio-Pleistocene global sea-level reconstruction, which extends beyond 5 Myr (de Boer et al., 2014). The public 100-yr series was interpolated to 1-kyr resolution. The same six metrics were evaluated for all 94 orientation chronologies, with same-duration Pleistocene windows as the primary background and the full record as a secondary sensitivity. No global sea-level result was interpreted as local Ryukyu/Taiwan land connectivity.

## Mid-Pleistocene timing and lineage-diversification context

Because the central orientation chronology lies near 0.8 Ma, we quantified rather than narratively assumed overlap with Mid-Pleistocene timing. For all 94 chronology pairs we recorded whether the branch spanned exactly 0.800 Ma or overlapped successively narrower bands around 0.8 Ma. High overlap was treated as chronology context, not an independent environmental test.

Published *Cirsium* studies were also used to distinguish lineage-level context from trait-specific cause. Moreyra et al. (2025) place the dominant Japanese radiation in the Pleistocene following dispersal to Japan, while Chang et al. (2025, 2026) resolve additional Japanese, Ryukyu and Taiwanese divergences during Quaternary climatic and island reorganization. These results motivate a historical setting of dispersal and isolation, but they were not used as evidence that a particular climate or geographic process caused a capitulum transition.

## Dated sister contexts for other capitulum traits

The available public chronology was audited for flower colour, phyllary architecture and floral display without converting dated sister differences into transition ages. Two white-lineage systems—*C. kawakamii* in Taiwan and *C. brevicaule* in Arenicola—provide conditional dated terminal-lineage contexts, but ancestral colour and the exact position of the colour change remain unresolved. Dated Taiwan and Arenicola phyllary/display contrasts are extant sister differences rather than reconstructed transitions. Published range ages involving *C. dipsacolepis* and *C. lineare* were retained as distribution-process context only and were not assigned to stickiness changes.

## Transparency and generative-AI assistance

All admission rules, evidence classes, scenario rows, negative results and claim boundaries are stored in machine-readable ledgers and analysis contracts. `Not_evaluable` denotes insufficient historical linkage and is not equivalent to biological absence. Generative AI assisted with code and prose development. All outputs were reviewed against primary sources, frozen contracts and machine-readable results; AI did not determine botanical states, alter frozen decision rules or generate underlying observational data.

# Results

## Capitulum modules differentiated repeatedly but at unequal evolutionary depths

All three completed discrete histories required repeated change (Fig. 1). Orientation required six changes on the maximum-likelihood tree and four to six across 1,000 bootstrap topologies, with a median of five. Phyllary posture required exactly three changes on the maximum-likelihood tree and every bootstrap topology. Stickiness required exactly five changes across the same topology ensemble.

Their temporal geometry differed. The orientation UFBoot median relative-depth envelope was 0.795–0.994, allowing both internal and terminal placements. Phyllary posture extended furthest toward broader internal lineages, with a median envelope of 0.695–1.000. Stickiness was more tightly shallow-biased, with a median envelope of approximately 0.937–0.954. Thus similar recurrence counts did not imply similar evolutionary depth.

Pairwise localization did not support one synchronized discrete history. Zero of three trait pairs—orientation–phyllary, orientation–stickiness and phyllary–stickiness—passed the robust shared-transition-localization rule after branch-aware and equal-branch topology sensitivity (Fig. 1). This constrains a universal synchronized capitulum history but does not prove complete independence among modules.

## Historical recurrence was far better resolved than calendar-time causation

The event-identifiability ledger exposed a sharp loss of resolution from transition history to transition cause (Fig. 2). Only the core-*Nipponocirsium* orientation event passed the full gate of a reconciled trait transition, calendar constraints, alternative palaeolocation scenarios and a commensurate historical environmental series.

Flower colour supplied three conditional dated branch envelopes, including white *C. kawakamii* and *C. brevicaule* lineages, but transition timing and ancestral direction remained conditional. Four phyllary/display comparisons were dated sister phenotypes rather than reconstructed transitions. Two stickiness-related public ages described range processes rather than trait-changing branches. A targeted public recovery audit located no machine-readable dated Newick or posterior tree set that could calendar-bound the remaining Japan38 parsimony changes. Consequently, repeated orientation, phyllary and stickiness histories could not be converted into repeated calendar-time event series.

## A coherent central climate trajectory disappeared across the full orientation uncertainty envelope

The one dateable orientation event yielded 94 admissible chronology pairs and four palaeolocation scenarios, for 376 historical trajectories (Fig. 3). The central 0.79→0.74 Ma pair appeared strikingly coherent: BIO1, BIO4 and BIO15 decreased in all four regions, while BIO12 increased in Taiwan, the Ryukyu corridor and the broad East-Asian corridor but decreased in southern Japan.

That pattern did not survive the full uncertainty envelope. For every tested climate variable, regional distributions of signed change spanned both directions once the complete chronology grid was propagated. No BIO1, BIO4, BIO12 or BIO15 signed direction was therefore robust across chronology × palaeolocation uncertainty.

## No tested climate level, change magnitude or variability passed the robust event-window gate

The matched-window analysis gave the same decision for broader historical-regime metrics (Fig. 3). Across all four variables, none was consistently extreme across the chronology × palaeolocation envelope for mean environmental level, absolute endpoint change or temporal variability. The formal counts of robust variables were therefore zero for signed direction, level, absolute change and variability.

Sub-threshold tendencies nevertheless showed why a central chronology can be tempting. Across regional medians, BIO1 occupied the low side of same-duration background windows (approximately the 0.14 percentile position on a 0–1 scale) and its temporal SD occupied the high side (approximately 0.85). BIO4 level was also high-side (approximately 0.85). For the central 0.79→0.74 Ma pair, BIO1 level fell near the lower 5% and BIO1 temporal SD near the upper 5% in all four regions. These patterns are consistent with a cold and climatically variable Pleistocene regime context, but chronology uncertainty prevented classification as an extreme event-associated regime.

## Global sea-level dynamics did not identify the bounded orientation trigger

The Spratt–Lisiecki stack covered only 16 of the 94 admissible chronology pairs and therefore could not support a full-envelope decision. Within the central 50-kyr window, sea-level mean, SD, range, endpoint change and 1-kyr change metrics were not exceptional relative to same-duration windows.

The de Boer reconstruction provided complete 94/94 chronology coverage and independently tested the same range-reorganization idea (Fig. 4). No global eustatic metric remained consistently extreme across chronology. For the primary Pleistocene background, median scenario percentiles were 0.287 for mean sea level, 0.699 for SD, 0.714 for range, 0.784 for absolute endpoint change, 0.857 for mean absolute 1-kyr change and 0.807 for maximum 1-kyr change. Every metric retained a broad uncertainty envelope and was classified `unresolved_across_chronology`. The formal decision was `no_global_sea_level_metric_survives_full_chronology_gate`.

The central 0.79→0.74 Ma window itself had relatively high sea-level variability and short-term change-rate percentiles, but these tendencies did not persist across admissible chronologies. Global eustatic change therefore remains broad range context rather than an identified orientation trigger.

## Mid-Pleistocene overlap was chronology context rather than independent evidence

The orientation event is centered near 0.8 Ma, but this overlap was largely predetermined by the published node constraints. Fifty-six of 94 admissible chronology pairs spanned exactly 0.800 Ma, 90 overlapped 0.700–0.900 Ma, 78 overlapped 0.750–0.850 Ma and 71 overlapped 0.770–0.830 Ma. Mid-Pleistocene overlap therefore describes the age envelope but does not independently discriminate a causal mechanism.

## Trait modules occupy different historical-identifiability classes

The combined ledger placed capitulum modules in distinct historical classes (Fig. 5). Orientation had the most complete chain: repeated history, broad relative-depth placement and one calendar-bounded event, but its environmental trigger remained unresolved. Phyllary posture had a strong three-change history with relatively deep admissible placements but no dated reconstructed transition. Stickiness had an equally stable five-change history concentrated on shallow lineages, while available public dates concerned range rather than trait change. Flower colour supplied two biologically interesting dated white-lineage contexts but no fully identified repeated ancestral colour history or commensurate historical radiation series.

At the whole-capitulum level, the evidence therefore supports repeated but uneven assembly. The radiation context is well dated relative to trait history, whereas trait-specific environmental causes are not. Repeated phenotypic differentiation and repeated historical triggering are different empirical claims.

# Discussion

## A rapid radiation can preserve differentiation history more clearly than differentiation cause

The main result is an asymmetry of historical resolution. Orientation, phyllary posture and stickiness all show repeated differentiation, and their relative-depth profiles reveal distinct evolutionary architectures. Yet moving from “the trait changed” to “this environmental process accompanied the change” causes a sharp loss of identifiability. Only one orientation event could be bounded strongly enough for an event-level palaeoenvironmental test, and even there no climate or sea-level metric survived the full uncertainty gates.

This distinction matters for interpreting rapid radiations. Transition counts are often presented alongside broad environmental histories as if the two were automatically linked. Our results show why that shortcut is unsafe. A radiation can occur during a period of glacial oscillation, island fragmentation and ecological opportunity while the cause of a particular morphological transition remains unresolved. Lineage diversification context and trait differentiation trigger are related questions, but they are not the same estimand.

## Unequal evolutionary depth implies trait-specific assembly rather than one synchronized capitulum history

The three discrete traits differed not only in how often they changed but in where minimum histories could place those changes. Phyllary posture retained the deepest admissible histories, orientation spanned internal to terminal lineages, and stickiness was concentrated near the tips. These differences persist despite topological uncertainty and cannot be summarized by one “rate of capitulum evolution.”

The 0/3 shared-localization result reinforces this interpretation. It rejects the simplest synchronized-history model in which the same branches repeatedly reorganize all capitulum components. This does not establish developmental or genetic modularity, because common developmental constraints could still generate covariance without identical transition branches. It does show that historical explanations should be trait-specific before they are combined into a whole-capitulum model.

Stickiness is especially suggestive. Its five-change minimum is invariant across the topology ensemble, yet the admissible history is strongly shallow. Such a pattern is compatible with rapid lineage-specific reassembly and motivates local biotic or cost-based hypotheses, but no historical enemy-pressure series currently links those shallow changes to a selective agent. Conversely, phyllary posture can reach deeper lineages but currently lacks calendarized transition branches. The same historical framework therefore produces different next questions for different modules.

## The bounded orientation event does not support a single robust Pleistocene trigger

The central orientation chronology illustrates the risk of overinterpreting point estimates. A 0.79→0.74 Ma window yields an intuitively coherent pattern of cooling and shifting seasonality, and it lies in a climatically dynamic part of the Pleistocene. If only those central node ages were analysed, a plausible historical narrative would be easy to construct.

Propagating the published marginal intervals changes that conclusion. The sign of every tested climate trajectory becomes uncertain, and no environmental level, absolute change or temporal variability remains consistently extreme across both chronology and palaeolocation scenarios. The event does show a tendency toward low BIO1 and high BIO1 variability, with especially extreme central-pair values, but this is better treated as a regime-context hypothesis than as evidence of a trigger.

Independent sea-level analyses place a similar boundary on geographic explanations. The empirical late-Pleistocene stack cannot cover most admissible parent ages, whereas the model-based de Boer series provides complete coverage. Neither identifies a robust sea-level level, variability or change-rate signal across chronology. This does not exclude local island connectivity as important to lineage history: global eustatic sea level is not a bathymetric reconstruction, and paleogeographic effects can be spatially nonlinear. It does show that a broad claim that “an unusual sea-level episode triggered orientation differentiation” is not supported by the current public evidence.

## Mid-Pleistocene timing is a meaningful background, not a causal result

The orientation branch overlaps Mid-Pleistocene timing under nearly all admissible chronology scenarios. That is useful for placing the event in the broader history of East-Asian *Cirsium*, but the overlap is not statistically independent of the node constraints used to define the branch. Calling the Mid-Pleistocene Transition a trigger would therefore recycle the chronology into its own explanation.

A more defensible interpretation is hierarchical. Pleistocene climatic oscillations, dispersal and island fragmentation provide a well-supported setting for lineage diversification (Moreyra et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025, 2026). Within that setting, capitulum traits differentiated repeatedly at different relative depths. The specific environmental event that caused any one transition, however, remains a separate and more demanding inference.

## Non-identifiability narrows rather than ends the causal programme

Failure to recover a robust trigger is not equivalent to evidence that environment was irrelevant. Several biological scenarios remain compatible with the public data. A transition may have responded to a local exposure not represented at 1° resolution; biotic interactions may have dominated an abiotic climate signal; the relevant selective variable may have been an extreme event rather than the tested mean/variance metrics; introgression or ancestral polymorphism may complicate a simple transition model; or the true event time may occupy a narrow portion of the broad branch interval.

The analysis nevertheless eliminates weaker shortcuts. Present descendants cannot be substituted for ancestral environments, a lineage split cannot be substituted for a trait-transition date, and a central node-age pair cannot override the full uncertainty envelope. These constraints identify precisely which new data would improve causal inference: a machine-readable dated tree or posterior linked to the focal concepts, event-specific palaeogeography, direct environmental exposure at flowering, and ancestry-resolved tests of trait function and reproductive fitness.

Flower colour illustrates a parallel boundary. Two white-lineage systems are dated at the lineage level, but ancestral colour and exact transition positions remain conditional. Their existence is valuable for comparative work, yet a repeated historical colour trigger should not be inferred until the phenotype history and a commensurate historical optical environment are both resolved. Phyllary posture and stickiness are even clearer cases where strong recurrence precedes driver identification.

## Implications for evolutionary explanations of complex reproductive structures

The capitulum results support a general distinction between **assembly history** and **historical cause**. Assembly history can often be reconstructed from extant states and a phylogenetic ensemble even when environmental causation cannot. Treating these as separate inference levels is especially useful for complex reproductive structures, where different modules may respond to rainfall, temperature, pollinators, herbivores, mechanical constraints or developmental covariance on different spatial and temporal scales.

Our results therefore favour a trait-specific historical programme over a universal capitulum syndrome. The evidence supports recurrent reassembly and unequal depth, while common environmental triggering remains unconfirmed. This intermediate conclusion is stronger than either assuming one adaptive syndrome or declaring all modules independent: it identifies which components have robust histories, which have dateable events and where the causal chain currently breaks.

## Limitations

Several limitations are structural. The Japan38 scaffold is a substitutions-per-site phylogram rather than a chronogram. The core-*Nipponocirsium* event combines marginal node-age constraints from separate studies and its 94 chronology pairs are deterministic scenarios, not posterior samples. The four palaeolocation boxes are sensitivity regions rather than ancestral-area probabilities. PALEO-PGEM has 1° resolution and cannot represent fine mountain microclimate or local topographic exposure. The de Boer sea-level record is model-based and global, and the Spratt–Lisiecki empirical stack lacks coverage for most admissible parent ages. Neither reconstructs local island connectivity.

Trait-state histories are also species/concept-level compressions. Within-lineage polymorphism, introgression and unresolved taxonomy can alter ancestral-state inference. Minimum changes do not distinguish independent origins from reversals or sorting of ancestral variation. Finally, no analysis here measures a trait-to-function mechanism or reproductive fitness. Historical environmental alignment, even if recovered in future work, would remain observational until those causal links are tested.

# Conclusion

Capitulum diversity in a young East-Asian *Cirsium* radiation was assembled through repeated but temporally uneven differentiation. Orientation requires four to six minimum changes, phyllary posture exactly three and involucre stickiness exactly five, yet their relative-depth profiles differ and no discrete trait pair shares a robust transition-localization history. The historical chain becomes much less identifiable at the level of cause. Only one orientation event can presently be bounded by both calendar time and alternative palaeolocations, and neither directional climate change, environmental extremeness, change magnitude, temporal variability nor global eustatic sea-level dynamics survives the full uncertainty gates for that event. The strongest public-data inference is therefore that **phenotypic differentiation history is better resolved than the environmental trigger of differentiation**. Pleistocene climate and island reorganization provide a credible radiation context, but trait-specific causes and adaptive consequences require finer event timing, palaeogeography, mechanism and reproductive-fitness evidence.
